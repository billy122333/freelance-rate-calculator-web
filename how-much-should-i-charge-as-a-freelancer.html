Pricing Framework

How much should I charge as a freelancer?

If that question keeps turning into guesswork, the answer is usually not another random industry average. The better answer is a pricing floor built from your own workload, costs, and financial target.

Why generic pricing advice feels useless

Freelancers often hear advice like “charge what the market charges” or “double your old salary.” Those shortcuts can be useful references, but they do not know your capacity, your cost structure, or how many real billable hours you can sell.

A better pricing question to ask

Instead of asking only what other people charge, ask what your business needs in order to stay sustainable. That means pricing from the inside out.

  • How much take-home income do you need each month?
  • How many billable hours can you realistically sell?
  • What monthly costs support your freelance work?
  • How much uncertainty shows up in your projects?
Pricing confidence usually comes from structure, not courage. A repeatable pricing method makes it easier to quote without freezing or apologizing.

When to charge more than your floor

Your internal floor is not the same thing as your public ceiling. Once your base economics work, you can raise pricing for urgency, strategic value, complexity, decision risk, or heavy collaboration.

How to stop copying random averages

Market averages can help you sanity-check your numbers, but they should not replace your own model. If your rate only works in a perfect month, it probably does not work.

Use a calculator before the next quote

The easiest next step is to plug your real numbers into a calculator, then compare the result against your usual quoting habit. If the gap is big, that gap is probably where your margin has been leaking.

Quick takeaway

A good freelance price is not pulled from the air. It is built from your target income, workload reality, business overhead, and risk.
